# Pork Miso Soboro Filling

## Title & Overview
This is a **filling-only** recipe for onigiri: a dry, compact pork miso soboro meant to be paired with your separate rice-and-shaping base. It is built from standard niku miso and soboro technique, but tightened for your use case: no onion or garlic solids, a fine ground texture for easy stuffing, and a low-moisture finish so it freezes and reheats more cleanly than a saucier pork mixture. Soboro is traditionally cooked into small seasoned crumbles or "pebbles," and the low-ish-FODMAP aroma strategy here is ginger plus garlic-infused oil rather than onion or garlic solids. [1][2][3][4]

## Yield & Timing
- **Yield:** about 12 fill portions, about 2 tsp each; roughly 1 1/2 cups total filling
- **Active Prep:** 10 min
- **Inactive Prep / Hands-Off / Rest:** 10 min
- **Cook:** 10 to 12 min
- **Total:** 30 to 32 min
- **Make-ahead:** Refrigerate for up to 3 to 4 days. Freeze in small portions for up to 3 months safely; for best texture in this specific filling use-case, I would use it within 1 month. Soboro is a good freezer candidate, and USDA leftovers guidance supports the broader cold-storage window. [3][5][6]

## Ingredients
- 1 lb ground pork
- 1 Tbsp finely grated fresh ginger
- 1 tsp avocado oil or garlic-infused avocado oil
- 1 tsp toasted sesame oil
- 2 Tbsp + 2 tsp red miso or awase miso
- 1 Tbsp + 1 tsp soy sauce or tamari
- 2 Tbsp + 2 tsp mirin
- 2 Tbsp + 2 tsp sugar
- 2 Tbsp + 2 tsp sake or water
- 2 3/4 tsp cornstarch or potato starch

## Allergy & Dietary Notes
- **Contains:** soy, sesame
- **Easy swaps (keep technique intact):**
  - Soy sauce -> tamari -> same method, gluten-free result
  - Garlic-infused avocado oil -> plain avocado oil -> slightly less aroma, still works
  - Red miso -> awase miso -> milder and slightly rounder flavor
  - Sake -> water -> slightly less depth, same texture path
- **Hard swaps (not recommended / major change):**
  - Onion or garlic solids -> breaks the intended low-ish-FODMAP direction; Monash specifically notes that fructans in onion and garlic are not suitable in the elimination phase, while infused oil is a workable route because fructans are not oil-soluble. [4][8]
  - Very lean pork -> drier, less cohesive filling after chilling
- **Diet toggles (optional; only if relevant to the dish):**
  - Low-sodium: reduce soy sauce to 1 Tbsp, use awase miso, and lean harder on ginger for lift
  - High-protein: use a slightly larger fill amount per onigiri rather than changing the sauce balance
  - Low-FODMAP: use garlic-infused oil and no onion or garlic solids; scallion greens are a more compatible aromatic than white onion if you ever choose to add any in a separate variation. [4][8]
  - Gluten-free: use tamari and confirm your miso is gluten-free

## Quick Overview
- **What you are making:** A pork-based niku miso filling for stuffing into onigiri, not a full rice-ball recipe.
- **Texture and doneness cues:** The target is fine, moist crumbles that hold together lightly, not loose mince in sauce. Soboro is classically cooked by breaking the meat up into small pieces or "pebbles" as it cooks. [1][2][7]
- **Workflow:** Mix the sauce first, brown and finely crumble the pork, reduce until nearly dry, cool, then portion for your separate onigiri build.
- **Make-ahead:** This is a strong make-ahead component; soboro keeps well and freezes well when portioned cleanly. [3][5]
- **Reheat plan:** For assembly, use it chilled, cool, or just barely warm; do not reheat it until piping hot before stuffing.
- **Primary risks:** Leaving the filling wet, using too much oil, or not breaking the pork down finely enough. A practical discussion rule of thumb for onigiri fillings is to avoid anything with too much liquid. [1][2][9]

## Gather & Stage
- Measure all ingredients before heating the pan.
- In a small bowl, stir together the miso, soy sauce or tamari, mirin, sake or water, sugar, and cornstarch or potato starch until smooth.
- Set out the **Ceramic nonstick pans** and, if using, the **Sheet / baking tray, 13x9, metal, light** for quick cooling.
- Keep a spatula or spoon ready for constant breaking-up once the pork hits the pan.
- **Checkpoint:** The sauce is fully mixed, smooth, and free of miso lumps. Dissolving the miso fully before it hits the pan is a recurring technique note in niku miso recipes. [1]

## Instructions

1. Mix the sauce
   - **Whisk** the miso, soy sauce or tamari, mirin, sake or water, sugar, and cornstarch or potato starch together in a small bowl.
     - Stir until the miso is fully dissolved and the starch is evenly dispersed. JOC explicitly uses this dissolve-first approach so the final meat sauce does not contain clumps of undissolved miso. [1]
     - Set the bowl next to the stove and stir it once more right before adding it to the pan.
     - **Checkpoint:** The mixture looks smooth and pourable, with no visible miso streaks.

2. Brown and crumble the pork
   - **Heat** the **Ceramic nonstick pans** over **medium heat**.
     - Add the avocado oil or garlic-infused avocado oil and the toasted sesame oil.
     - Add the ground pork and **saute** for 4 to 5 minutes, breaking it up constantly into very small crumbles. Japanese Cooking 101 describes soboro as ground meat cooked while being broken into small pieces or pebbles, and JOC gives the same practical cue to separate the meat into small crumbles as it cooks. [1][2][7]
     - Add the grated ginger once the pork is mostly separated and continue cooking until the meat is nearly no longer pink.
     - **Checkpoint:** The pork is finely crumbled and sizzling lightly rather than sitting in a pool of moisture.
     - *If the pork starts steaming instead of sizzling -> spread it out more and stop stirring for 20 to 30 seconds -> cue it's fixed: moisture starts cooking off and you hear a clearer sizzle.*
     - *If the pork is still in larger clumps after several minutes -> press and scrape with the spatula more aggressively -> cue it's fixed: the meat looks like small, even pebbles instead of chunks.*

3. Add the sauce and reduce
   - **Add** the sauce mixture to the pan when the pork is nearly cooked through.
     - Stir well, then **reduce** over **medium** to **medium-low heat** for 3 to 5 minutes, stirring often, until the sauce clings tightly to the meat.
     - The endpoint here is intentionally drier than a general-purpose bowl topping: no pooling liquid, no glossy puddles, and the spoon should leave a short path through the mixture before the crumbles settle back. That slight tightening is intentional because your filling has to sit inside rice and then freeze well. [1][3][9]
     - **Checkpoint:** The filling gathers into compact, seasoned crumbles and no longer looks saucy.
     - *If the mixture still looks loose or glossy -> keep **reducing** in 30-second increments -> cue it's fixed: the meat sits in moist crumbles rather than flowing.*
     - *If it starts catching too hard before it is reduced enough -> lower the heat and add 1 tsp water -> cue it's fixed: it loosens briefly, then tightens back around the pork without scorching.*

4. Cool, portion, and store
   - **Spread** the filling on the **Sheet / baking tray, 13x9, metal, light** or on a plate in a thin layer.
     - Let it cool for 5 to 10 minutes before portioning. For stuffing onigiri, cool, chilled, or barely warm filling handles better than piping hot filling.
     - Portion into about 12 small servings, about 2 tsp each, or store as one batch if you will weigh or spoon from it later.
     - Taste-adjust loop: salt -> add a few drops of soy only if the filling tastes flat; sweetness -> add a pinch more sugar only if the miso tastes sharp; aroma -> add a little more ginger only if it needs lift; heat -> add chile only if your separate onigiri base wants it.
     - **Checkpoint:** The finished filling tastes slightly stronger than you would want to eat by itself, which is correct because rice will mute it.

## Common Issues
- **Too wet for filling:** It was not reduced far enough -> keep cooking over medium-low -> test by dragging a spoon through the pan
- **Too greasy:** The pork rendered more fat than expected -> reduce slightly longer or blot lightly before cooling -> test by pressing a spoonful on a plate

## Make-Ahead Notes
- Fridge plan:
  - Stop after Step 4: cool promptly and refrigerate in a covered container
  - Holds: 3 to 4 days. USDA leftovers guidance supports this window. [5][6]
  - Serve/Reheat: use chilled, or warm very lightly if needed for easier scooping
- Freezer plan:
  - Best freeze point: after Step 4, in small onigiri-sized portions
  - Portioning: freeze in 12 small packets or spooned mounds
  - Freeze: up to 3 months safely; for best filling texture, I would use within 1 month. [3][5]
  - Thaw: overnight in the fridge, or microwave in short bursts until just loosened
  - Reheat: do not overheat before stuffing; warm or cool filling is easier to handle than hot filling

## Reheat Plan
- **Best method:** microwave -> 15-second bursts from chilled or frozen-thawed -> until loosened and just warm, not piping hot
- **Texture reset:** stir once after each burst so the edges do not overcook while the center stays cold
- *If the filling gets oily after reheating -> stir and let it sit 1 minute -> cue it's fixed: the fat re-coats the meat instead of separating.*
- *If the center stays cold while the edges heat too fast -> lower the microwave power and use one extra burst -> cue it's fixed: the filling loosens evenly.*

## Troubleshooting
- Filling leaks out of rice later -> It was too wet -> Reduce it further before storing -> It scoops as compact crumbs
- Filling tastes too intense by itself -> That is normal for onigiri filling -> Test it inside rice before changing it -> It tastes balanced once paired with rice
- Filling feels stiff from the fridge -> Fat firmed up -> Warm in very short bursts -> It loosens without turning greasy
- Filling tastes flat after chilling -> Cold muted the aroma -> Warm briefly or add a tiny bit more ginger -> It tastes vivid again
