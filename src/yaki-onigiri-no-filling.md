# Base Soy-Glazed Yaki Onigiri

## Title & Overview
This is a plain, freezer-friendly yaki onigiri base: Japanese short-grain white rice cooked in the rice cooker, shaped while still warm, lightly browned in a skillet, then brushed with a thin soy glaze. It is intentionally simple and intentionally unfilled, because plain yaki onigiri are easier to shape, less fragile in the pan, and more reliable for freeze/reheat than stuffed versions. The glaze is kept light so the rice still tastes like rice and does not turn overly salty on reheat. Filling is handled in a separate companion recipe; this base recipe is for the rice, shaping, glazing, pan-frying, and freezing workflow only. [1]

- **Yield:** 18 to 24 small onigiri
- **Active Prep:** 35 minutes
- **Inactive Prep / Hands-Off / Rest:** 30 minutes
- **Cook:** 55 minutes
- **Total:** about 2 hours
- **Make-ahead:** Best frozen after pan-frying and cooling. Wrap individually and freeze up to 1 month. Reheat in the microwave until hot, then re-crisp briefly in a skillet if you want the exterior back. [2]

## Equipment & Tools

- Cast iron skillet - best for a firmer crust on the broad faces
- Carbon steel skillet - acceptable substitute; heats faster and needs a bit more attention
- fine-mesh sieve - for draining the rice thoroughly before cooking
- Sheet / baking tray 13x9 - for staging, cooling, and wrapping the finished onigiri

## Ingredients
- 3 cups Japanese short-grain white rice, rinsed well **[pre-prep]**
- 3 1/3 cups water, or enough to reach the 4-cup white-rice line in your rice cooker
- 1 tsp kosher salt, for lightly salting hands or mold
- 2 tbsp soy sauce
- 1 tbsp mirin
- 1 tsp granulated sugar
- 1 tsp toasted sesame oil
- 1 to 2 tsp avocado oil, for the skillet only as needed

## Allergy & Dietary Notes
- **Contains:** soy, alcohol
- **Easy swaps (keep technique intact):**
  - mirin -> 2 tsp water plus 1/2 tsp sugar -> slightly less gloss, but the glaze still browns properly
  - toasted sesame oil -> avocado oil -> less nutty aroma, but cleaner and still functional
- **Hard swaps (not recommended / major change):**
  - long-grain rice -> does not hold like onigiri rice -> use Japanese short-grain white rice
  - heavy glaze coating -> burns and gets harsh in the pan -> keep the glaze thin and brush it on only after the first crust forms
- **Diet toggles (optional; only if relevant to the dish):**
  - Low-sodium: use low-sodium soy sauce, skip the hand salt, and glaze only one broad face
  - Gluten-free: use gluten-free tamari in place of soy sauce
- **Cross-contact note (optional):**
  - Check soy sauce and mirin labels if gluten matters

## Quick Overview
- **What you are making:** Plain yaki onigiri base, not a stuffed onigiri and not the filling recipe.
- **Texture and doneness cues:** The rice should be warm and cohesive when shaped; the outside should be lightly browned and lightly glossy, not dark or sauce-heavy.
- **Workflow:** Cook the rice in the rice cooker, shape while warm, dry-pan the rice balls until set, then brush on the glaze and finish briefly in the pan. [1][2]
- **Make-ahead:** These freeze well once cooled and individually wrapped. [2]
- **Reheat plan:** Microwave until hot all the way through, then re-crisp briefly in a skillet if you want the yaki texture back. [2]
- **Primary risks:** Rice too cool before shaping, flipping too early, and brushing on too much glaze too soon.

## Gather & Stage
- Measure and group all ingredients by phase; place them within reach.
- Set out the Cuckoo Rice Cooker, Cast iron skillet, fine-mesh sieve, and Sheet / baking tray 13x9.
- Complete all **[pre-prep]** items that affect cook timing or sequencing.
- Mix the glaze in a small bowl: soy sauce, mirin, sugar, and toasted sesame oil.
- Set out a bowl of water and the kosher salt for shaping.
- Pull out plastic wrap for the freeze step.
- **Checkpoint:** everything needed for Step 1 is ready and within reach.

## Instructions

1. Cook the rice
   - **Rinse** the rice in several changes of cold water until the water is much less cloudy, then **drain** it very well in the fine-mesh sieve.
     - Transfer the drained rice to the Cuckoo Rice Cooker.
     - Add 3 1/3 cups water, or use the white-rice water line for 4 rice-cooker cups if your inner pot has one. Japanese short-grain white rice in a rice cooker generally wants about 1 to 1.1 water by volume, plus a 20 to 30 minute soak. [1]
     - **Soak** the rice for 20 to 30 minutes before starting the cooker if your machine does not already build that into the cycle. Then **cook** on the regular white-rice setting. [1]
     - When the cycle ends, **rest** the rice 10 minutes if your cooker does not include a steaming phase.
     - **Fluff** gently so the grains release steam without getting mashed.
     - **Checkpoint:** You are ready to proceed when the rice is tender, glossy, and still warm.
     - *If the rice looks wet on top, then spread it on the Sheet / baking tray 13x9 for 2 to 3 minutes; cue it's fixed: surface steam is reduced but the rice still clings together.*

2. Shape the onigiri
   - **Shape** the rice while it is still warm, not cold.
     - Set a bowl of water and the kosher salt next to your station. Wet your hands lightly, then rub a very small amount of salt across your palms.
     - For small pieces, use about 1/4 to 1/3 cup warm rice per onigiri.
     - Press into thick triangles or rounded triangles. Keep the pressure firm enough that they hold together, but do not crush them dense.
     - Transfer each shaped piece to the tray as you work.
     - This base recipe is intentionally plain. Filling is handled in a separate companion recipe; do not stuff this batch. [3]
     - **Checkpoint:** You are ready to proceed when each piece lifts cleanly and holds its shape.
     - *If the rice starts cracking, then it has cooled too much; cue it's fixed after you cover it for a few minutes or microwave it briefly until warm again.*
     - *If the rice sticks badly to your hands, then re-wet your hands lightly; cue it's fixed: the surface smooths without tearing.*

3. Pan-fry the plain onigiri
   - **Pan-fry** the shaped onigiri over **medium-low heat** in the Cast iron skillet.
     - Start with a dry pan or only a very thin film of avocado oil if you are worried about sticking.
     - Place the onigiri in the skillet with enough room to turn them cleanly. Do not crowd them.
     - **Cook** the first broad face for 3 to 5 minutes, until lightly browned and dry enough to release.
     - **Flip** and cook the second broad face for another 3 to 5 minutes, until the outside feels more set and less delicate. [2]
     - For this base batch, you only need to brown the two broad faces. Full all-sides grilling is optional and slower.
     - **Checkpoint:** You are ready to proceed when the onigiri feel lightly firm on the outside and release cleanly from the skillet.
     - *If one tears while turning, then it was flipped too early; cue it's fixed when you leave the next batch undisturbed until browned before moving it.*

4. Glaze and finish
   - **Brush** the glaze on only after the first crust has formed.
     - Stir the glaze so the sugar is evenly dissolved.
     - Lower the skillet to **low heat**.
     - Brush a thin coat of glaze on one browned face and **cook** 20 to 40 seconds, until glossy.
     - **Flip**, brush the second face, and cook another 20 to 40 seconds.
     - Keep the coating light. The point is a thin savory lacquer, not a wet sauce layer. [2]
     - **Checkpoint:** You are ready to proceed when the surface is lightly glossy and smells toasty, not burnt.
     - *If the glaze darkens too fast, then the heat is too high or the layer is too thick; cue it's fixed after lowering the heat and brushing more thinly.*

5. Cool, wrap, and freeze
   - **Cool** the finished onigiri on the tray until they stop actively steaming, then **wrap** each one individually in plastic wrap.
     - Freeze in a single layer first if convenient, then transfer to a bag or container.
     - Filling is handled in another recipe; this base recipe ends here with the plain glazed rice cakes. [2][4]
     - Taste-adjust loop: if the batch needs more salt, add a little more soy to the next batch of glaze; if it tastes too salty, brush more lightly next time and skip the hand salt.
     - **Checkpoint:** Final dish is a compact, plain yaki onigiri base with a lightly browned exterior and a light glaze.

## Common Issues
- **Onigiri crack while shaping:** Rice cooled too much -> warm it slightly and keep it covered -> quick check: it presses smooth again. [3]
- **They stick in the pan:** You flipped too early or used too much glaze too soon -> wait for the crust first -> quick check: the spatula slides under the browned face.
- **They taste too salty on reheat:** Glaze was too heavy -> brush more thinly next batch -> quick check: the rice flavor still comes through.

## Make-Ahead Notes
- Fridge plan:
  - Stop after Step 5: cool, wrap individually, and refrigerate
  - Holds: up to 2 days
  - Serve/Reheat: microwave until hot, then re-crisp briefly in the skillet if desired. [2]
- Freezer plan:
  - Best freeze point: Step 5, after pan-frying and cooling
  - Portioning: 1 onigiri per wrap; then bag together
  - Freeze: up to 1 month
  - Thaw: optional; you can microwave from chilled or frozen
  - Reheat: microwave until hot, then return to the skillet briefly to restore some crust. Reheated leftovers should reach 165 deg F. [2][5]

## Reheat Plan
- **Best method:** microwave -> 45 to 60 seconds for chilled or 75 to 120 seconds for frozen, depending on size -> until the center is hot and steaming
- **Texture reset:** skillet -> **medium-low heat** -> 30 to 60 seconds per broad face -> cue: the surface dries slightly and regains some crust. [2]
- *If the outside is warm but the center is still cool, then microwave 15 to 20 seconds more before re-crisping; cue it's fixed: the center feels hot when pressed or checked.*

## Troubleshooting
- Rice feels dry or crumbly -> too little water or insufficient soak -> use the cooker line or the 1 to 1.1 water ratio and soak 20 to 30 minutes -> shaped rice holds together. [1]
- Onigiri collapse in the pan -> not pressed firmly enough or moved too early -> shape more firmly and wait for browning before flipping -> clean turn with no tearing. [2]
- Glaze tastes harsh -> heat too high or glaze layer too thick -> lower to low heat and brush more lightly -> the finish tastes savory and toasty instead of burnt.

## Troubleshooting Table

| Symptom | Likely Cause | Quick Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Falls apart while turning | Crust not set yet | Leave the first face down longer before flipping |
| Too dark before heated through | Heat too high after glazing | Lower to low heat and use a thinner coat |
| Dry after reheating | Overheated in microwave | Shorter microwave time, then skillet finish |

## Geometry Notes
- Pan/pot size used: Cuckoo Rice Cooker; Cast iron skillet
- Target thickness / layer depth: about 1 to 1 1/4 in thick
- Crowding rule: leave at least a little turning room around each piece; do not pack the skillet edge to edge

## Scaling & Batch Size
- Scaling notes: Halve the rice and glaze for a smaller trial batch.
- Batch strategy: If your rice cooker's white-rice max line is below 4 rice-cooker cups, cook the rice in two rounds rather than overfilling the pot.
- Pan swaps: Carbon steel skillet works well, but monitor it more closely because it responds faster to heat changes.

## Special Notes

## Variations
1. **Soy-only minimalist version:** Skip the mirin, sugar, and sesame oil and brush with a very thin coat of soy sauce only. This is the leanest, cleanest version and the easiest one to judge for salt level.

2. **Miso-brushed version:** Replace the soy glaze with a very thin miso glaze and cook it even more gently. It eats darker and more savory, but the glaze burns faster than the soy version, so keep the coat extremely thin. [6]

3. **Freeze-first workflow:** Shape plain, unsalted onigiri, wrap, and freeze them first, then defrost and turn them into yaki onigiri later. This is a workable plan-ahead path if you want to batch the rice base separately from the pan-fry day. [4]

## Safety & Correctness Notes
This base recipe keeps the rice plain and applies the sauce after the crust forms. That is intentional: it is more beginner-safe, more pan-stable, and more freezer-friendly than trying to start with a filled or heavily seasoned rice ball.
